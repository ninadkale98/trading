# 3. Valuation: Is a Stock Cheap or Expensive?

**Big idea:** A low share **price** does not mean a stock is **cheap**.
A $10 stock can be very expensive and a $1,000 stock can be cheap. What matters
is how much you pay **for the profits** you get.

## Market cap (market capitalization)

**Formula:** `Share price × Total shares`
**In plain words:** the price to buy the **whole company** at today's stock price.

**Intel:** $56.81 × 5.3B shares ≈ **$302B**

| Size | Market cap |
|---|---|
| Mega cap | > $200B |
| Large cap | $10B – $200B |
| Mid cap | $2B – $10B |
| Small cap | < $2B |

Our scan only looks at companies above **$10B**. They're more stable, easier to buy
and sell, and better covered by analysts.

## Shares outstanding and float

- **Shares outstanding** = all shares that exist (Intel: about 5.3B).
- **Float** = shares available to trade publicly (excludes insiders' locked shares).
- **Dilution** = the company issues new shares, so each existing share owns a
  smaller slice. That's usually bad for shareholders.

## P/E ratio (price-to-earnings)

**Formula:** `Share price ÷ EPS over the last 12 months`
**In plain words:** how many dollars you pay for **$1 of yearly profit**.
It's also the number of years of current profits it would take to "pay back" the price.

**Example:** Stock at $100, EPS of $5 a year → P/E = **20**. You pay $20 for every $1
of profit.

| P/E | Rough meaning |
|---|---|
| < 10 | Cheap, or the market expects trouble |
| 15–25 | Normal for a mature company |
| 30–60 | Expensive, so the market expects strong growth |
| Negative | The company **lost money**, so P/E is meaningless |

**Intel:** P/E = **−21.85** (from Robinhood). It's negative because Intel lost about
$12B over the last 12 months, mostly from the $11B loss in Q2. A negative P/E
doesn't mean "cheap". It means the measure doesn't work here.

## Forward P/E

**Formula:** `Share price ÷ EPS expected over the next 12 months`
**Why it's useful:** It looks at the **future**, which is what the stock price is
betting on.

**From our scan:**

| Stock | Forward P/E | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| EOG Resources | 8.8 | Cheap; the market expects oil profits to fall |
| Chevron | 13.7 | Normal for an oil major |
| Texas Instruments | 24.4 | Paying up for quality and recovery |

**Intel, a rough estimate:** Q4 guided EPS of about $0.25 × 4 quarters ≈ $1.00 a year.
$56.81 ÷ $1.00 ≈ **57×**. That's expensive, even after the 40% drop. This is why
"the price fell a lot" doesn't automatically mean "it's cheap now".

## PEG ratio

**Formula:** `P/E ÷ Expected yearly earnings growth (%)`
**In plain words:** P/E adjusted for growth. A P/E of 40 can be fine if profits are
growing 40% a year.

| PEG | Rough meaning |
|---|---|
| < 1 | Possibly cheap for its growth |
| ~1 | Fairly priced |
| > 2 | Expensive for its growth |

## P/B ratio (price-to-book)

**Book value** = what the company owns minus what it owes (assets − debts).
**P/B** = Market cap ÷ Book value.

**Intel:** P/B = **2.4**. Investors pay $2.40 for every $1 of net assets.
P/B matters most for companies with heavy assets, like banks, factories (Intel)
and steel (Nucor).

## Dividend and dividend yield

- **Dividend** = cash the company pays shareholders, usually every quarter.
- **Dividend yield** = yearly dividend ÷ share price.
  Example: $4 a year on a $100 stock = 4% yield.

**Intel:** it **suspended** its dividend in 2024 to save cash for building factories.
A dividend cut is usually a sign of financial pressure.

## EPS growth and revenue growth

Valuation always comes with growth. A stock with a high P/E is only worth it if
profits grow fast enough to "grow into" the price.

---

## Quick checklist

1. **Market cap:** how big is it?
2. **P/E or forward P/E:** what am I paying per $1 of profit?
3. **Compare** with competitors and the company's own history, not with random stocks.
4. **Growth:** does the growth rate justify the P/E?
5. **Remember:** a falling price ≠ cheap. Check whether the profits are falling too.
