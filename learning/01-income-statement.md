# 1. How a Company Makes Money (the Income Statement)

The **income statement** (also called the "P&L", profit and loss) shows money coming
in and money going out over a period, usually a quarter (3 months) or a year.

## The lemonade stand

You sell 100 cups of lemonade at $2 each.

| Line | Amount | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue** | $200 | All money from sales (100 × $2) |
| − Cost of goods sold (COGS) | −$80 | Lemons, sugar, cups: the direct cost of making what you sold |
| **= Gross profit** | **$120** | What's left after making the product |
| − Operating expenses | −$70 | Stand rent, your helper's pay, ads: the cost of running the business |
| **= Operating income** | **$50** | Profit from the actual business |
| − Interest and taxes | −$15 | Loan interest, government's share |
| **= Net income** | **$35** | The final profit, "the bottom line" |

Each line takes away more costs. Net income is the money that really belongs to
the owners (shareholders).

---

## Revenue (also called "sales" or "top line")

**What:** Total money from selling products and services.
**Why it matters:** Growing revenue means more customers or higher prices. It's the
first sign of a growing business.
**Watch out:** Revenue can grow while the company still loses money.

**Intel example:** Q3 2026 revenue was **$16.5B**, up **21%** from $13.7B a year
earlier. That's strong growth.

## Year-over-year (YoY) growth

**What:** This quarter compared with the **same quarter last year**.
**Formula:** `(this year − last year) ÷ last year`
**Why YoY and not vs the previous quarter?** Many businesses are seasonal (for example,
more PC sales during the holidays), so comparing Q3 with Q3 is fair.

**Intel:** (16.5 − 13.7) ÷ 13.7 = **+21% YoY**

## Gross profit and gross margin

**Gross profit** = Revenue − Cost of making the product.
**Gross margin** = Gross profit ÷ Revenue, as a percentage.

**In plain words:** for every $1 of sales, how many cents are left after making the
product?

| Company type | Typical gross margin |
|---|---|
| Software | 70–90% (copying software costs almost nothing) |
| Chip designers (NVIDIA) | 60–75% |
| Chip makers with factories (Intel) | 35–60% |
| Supermarkets | 20–30% |

**Intel:**

| Quarter | Revenue | Gross profit | Gross margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q4 2025 | $13.7B | $4.9B | 36% |
| Q1 2026 | $13.6B | $5.3B | 39% |
| Q2 2026 | $16.1B | $6.5B | 40% |
| Q3 2026 | $16.5B | $7.1B | **43%** |
| Q4 2026 (guided) | $15–16B | — | **~38%** |

**Why investors care so much:** A falling margin means each sale is less profitable.
That can come from price cuts, higher costs (like memory prices) or factories
running below capacity. Intel guiding its margin from about 43% down to 38% was
one of the main reasons the stock crashed.

## Operating income and operating margin

**Operating income** = Gross profit − Operating expenses (research, salaries,
marketing, admin).
**Operating margin** = Operating income ÷ Revenue.

**In plain words:** how profitable the *core business* is, before interest and taxes.

**From our scan:** Texas Instruments (TXN) has a **41%** operating margin. Expeditors
(EXPD) has **10%**. Our scan requires above 10%, so we skip companies that barely
make money from their actual business.

## Net income and net margin

**Net income** = what's left after **all** costs, including interest, taxes and
one-time charges. It's also called "earnings" or "the bottom line".
**Net margin** = Net income ÷ Revenue.

**Intel shows why net income can be misleading:**

| Quarter | Revenue | Net income | Net margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q2 2026 | $16.1B | **−$11.0B** | −68% |
| Q3 2026 | $16.5B | **+$3.3B** | +20% |

Revenue was about the same, but profit swung by $14B. The difference came from
**one-time items**: write-downs, restructuring costs, gains or losses on
investments. These are real, but they don't repeat every quarter, so investors
often look at "adjusted" numbers too (see below).

## GAAP vs adjusted ("non-GAAP")

- **GAAP** = the official accounting rules. Every one-time charge is included.
- **Adjusted / non-GAAP** = the company removes items it calls one-time (layoff costs,
  write-downs, stock-based pay and so on) to show the "normal" business.

**Why it matters:** Wall Street estimates are usually for the **adjusted** number.
When a headline says Intel "earned $0.52 per share, beating $0.37", that's adjusted.
Be a little skeptical: companies choose what to adjust, and some "one-time" costs
happen every year.

## Free cash flow (bonus)

**What:** Cash the business actually generates minus what it spends on equipment
and factories (capital expenditure, "capex").
**Why it matters:** Accounting profit can be shaped by accounting choices, but cash
is harder to fake. Intel spends huge amounts building chip factories, so its free
cash flow has been weak even in profitable quarters.

---

## Quick checklist when you look at a company

1. Is **revenue growing** YoY?
2. Is **gross margin** stable or rising?
3. Is **operating margin** healthy for its industry?
4. Is **net income** positive, and if not, is it because of one-time items?
5. Is the trend over the **last 4–8 quarters** getting better or worse?
